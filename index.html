What the rating is

Every classified finish in the 1990–2026 races is turned into pairwise duels: each driver is compared with every other classified finisher of the same race, and the winner of each duel is whoever finished ahead. The model then fits two numbers per driver per duel — a driver skill and a car strength — so that predicted win rates match what actually happened. This is the Bradley–Terry model, the same statistical family as Elo.

The one idea that makes this work. The two drivers of one team share the same car, so inside a team the car term subtracts out and what is left is pure driver-versus-driver. Those teammate duels anchor every rating; results against the rest of the field fix how strong each car was. A driver is never rewarded for a fast car beyond the points that car's teammate also scored.

What each column means

  • Rating (Elo) — career skill on one scale. 400 points of Elo ≈ a 10:1 win probability in a duel. Each is shown with its 95% interval; short careers have wide ones.
  • Peak — the best rolling three-season skill, so a driver is judged on their best years, not their length.
  • Car — the average strength of the machinery they drove. Excess is rating minus car: how much of the result was them.
  • vs field — a z-score: how far above the average driver of their own seasons they sat. This is the era-fair column, since every era's field is judged against itself.
  • Consistency — how reliably a driver both finishes and reaches the front: a blend of finish rate and top-10 rate. It rewards a driver who is always there, not one who merely completes laps in last place.
  • Points — championship points, but normalized per season (a driver's haul divided by the champion's that year) so that the 9-6-4 scale of 1994 and the 25-18-15 scale of today mean the same thing. Raw totals are never compared across eras.
  • Confidence — how much evidence stands behind the number, from the count of duels. A long career raises confidence in the rating; it never raises the rating itself.

What does not decide the ranking

Career length is not a rating input. Starts, seasons and career totals appear in each card so you can read the record, but the OVR weights skill, peak, consistency, era-relative superiority, era-normalized points and teammate record — none of them volume. A driver with 40 brilliant races can outrank one with 300 ordinary ones. Where an exception looks odd — a short career rated very high, or a long one rated low — the peak and vs-field columns explain it, and the record columns show the raw totals.

Every team counts

The car list holds all 424 constructor-seasons that raced between 1990 and 2026, not only the ones a championship table lists. Entrants with no classified standing — Simtek 1994, Forti 1995–96, Minardi 1996–98 and the rest — still carry a car rating and still appear, marked NC. A season with no points is scored as zero, because that is what it was.

Who is rated

Every driver who took a race start between 1990 and 2026 is here — 216 drivers, from seven-time champions to one-off stand-ins. Drivers with a thin record (under 10 starts) carry a PROV badge: the model still rates them, but their interval is wide and the number should be read as provisional. Nobody who raced is hidden.

Rookies and thin samples

A rating built from a handful of races is a guess with wide error bars, and the model says so rather than hiding it. Any driver with a small sample carries a PROV badge: their OVR is provisional and their interval is wide. Two flags drive it — early career (debuted in the last two seasons) and small sample (under 20 starts). The badge is a caution, never a penalty: a rookie is not ranked down for being new, only marked as unfinished.

Driving style, from behaviour

Style tags are read off measurable behaviour, not reputation: a driver who beats teammates on Saturday more than Sunday is tagged a one-lap specialist; the reverse is a race-day racer; average grid-to-flag change marks an overtaker; mechanical versus driver-caused retirements separate a reliable finisher from a crash-prone one.

Limits, stated plainly

  • Point systems changed across the era (and points are therefore only used as a sanity check, not as a rating input).
  • Reliability was far worse in the 1990s, so career win and finish counts favour modern drivers; the Elo, vs-field and consistency columns are the fair comparison, career totals are not.
  • Pre-1990 careers appear only through 1990 onward for the rating, and through full career totals for the record.
  • Qualifying times exist only from 2006 (before that, a single best lap; from 2006, Q1/Q2/Q3), so Saturday pace is measured by grid position across the whole window.
  • The model reads results, not context: a driver in a bad car beaten by a teammate in the same bad car is judged fairly, but a driver asked to run a different strategy or develop a car is not credited for it.
  • No telemetry is used, because none exists for this span. Lap-by-lap speed, throttle, brake and sector data are not published as structured, complete records across 1990–2026; the finest public granularity is the fastest-lap time, which the model treats as a race outcome. Everything here is built from finishing positions, grid positions and retirement causes.
